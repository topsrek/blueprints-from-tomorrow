# Campaign: Baghdad 830 – The House of Wisdom

- **id:** `baghdad-830`
- **era guide:** `techtree/generated/islamic-830-baghdad.md` (compact fallback: `techtree/generated/islamic-830-baghdad/briefing.md`; source: `techtree/eras/islamic-830-baghdad.yaml`)
- **start date:** early spring 830 (Safar AH 215), before the caliph leaves for the frontier (adjustable)
- **hook:** accident (default) or mission from the future (option below)
- **recommended duration:** one year, or "until the caliph dies" (to 7 August 833)
- **language:** Arabic; Persian, Syriac and Greek among the scholars; with the language axis off, the
  player is understood

## Premise
Baghdad under al-Ma'mun, the son of Harun al-Rashid, who took the city from his own
brother in a siege that burned whole quarters. The caliph collects books, pays
translators, sends astronomers up to the roof of the Shammasiyya quarter to check
Ptolemy's tables, and argues theology the way other rulers hunt. He has a library,
the Khizanat al-Hikma, which later writers will call the House of Wisdom and turn
into a university that never existed. The real thing is a palace library, a few
librarians, a bench of copyists, and a city full of patrons who pay by the page for
Greek turned into Arabic.

This is the only campaign in which the player arrives *ahead of Europe* and finds the
world already reaching for much of what they know. Paper is made here. Indian numerals
with a sign for zero have just been written up by al-Khwarizmi, together with a new
art he calls al-jabr. There are hospitals, astrolabes, alembics, canals, Euclid in
Arabic. What is missing is subtler: lenses, printing, an experiment that may overrule
Galen, a clock that runs without water. And time: in three years the caliph will
order every judge to swear that the Quran was created, and then die of a fever on
the Byzantine frontier.

## Mission from the future (optional hook)
The message reads: *"The library will burn in four hundred years. What is in it now
does not have to wait that long. You will be returned in one year."* It does not say
what should leave the library, or for whom.

## Opening scene
The player wakes on a bench at the edge of the Suq al-Warraqin, the paper-sellers'
market on the west bank, a lane of booths of copyists, binders and book dealers. A boy
is beating rags in a trough down the lane; the smell is wet linen, lamp oil, river
mud and roasting meat. A copyist with ink to the wrist looks at the player's clothes,
then at their hands, and asks, in Arabic, whose house they belong to and whether they
can write a fair hand. Across the Tigris, on the far bank, the pontoon bridge is
crowded with soldiers loading the caliph's baggage for the summer campaign.

## Arc (loose, react to the player)
1. **Nobody** – a stranger without kin, patron or religion that anyone recognises has
   no place. Free or slave, Muslim or protected unbeliever, is the first thing anyone
   will decide about the player. First need: a household (a Christian physician, a
   paper dealer, a merchant, a Mu'tazilite teacher) and a claim to be useful.
2. **A use** – something small that earns a hearing: a quicker way to divide an
   inheritance, a remedy that works, a trick with paper or glass, an eclipse date.
3. **The scholars** – the way up runs through the people who serve the caliph's
   curiosity: al-Khwarizmi with his digits and his al-jabr, the astronomers checking
   the tables, Ibn Masawayh at the sickbed, young al-Kindi with his Greek books, the
   Banu Musa with their money. They are clever and they do not need the player; the
   player must offer something they can check.
4. **The caliph** – al-Ma'mun is curious, proud, impatient and absent every summer.
   He wants proofs, instruments, victories over Byzantium and an argument that wins
   the theological war at home. He is reachable in winter and through the men he
   trusts.
5. **The Mihna** – from spring 833 the judges and traditionists of Baghdad are made
   to declare the Quran created. Ahmad ibn Hanbal refuses. The player's patrons will
   be on one side or the other.
6. **The frontier** – in August 833 the caliph dies near Tarsus, his brother
   al-Mu'tasim takes over, and the court will soon move to Samarra with a new army of
   Turkish slave-soldiers. The player knows the date.

## Hard choices
- **Caliphal patronage and the Mihna.** The caliph is the most generous patron of
  knowledge in the world and the man who starts an inquisition. The rational
  theologians the player may find congenial are the ones putting judges to the test;
  the traditionists who resist are defending conscience against the state. Helping
  either side helps someone persecute someone.
- **Slavery.** Domestic slaves, singing girls, eunuchs, Zanj labourers in the
  southern marshes, and the Turkish boys being bought as soldiers. The player's host
  will own people. What the player does in small things counts (see
  `modules/content-guidelines.md`).
- **Weapons and open scholarship.** The same geometry measures the earth and aims a
  trebuchet; the same alembic makes rose water and better naphtha. Everything the
  player teaches can go north with the army against Byzantium, or to al-Mu'tasim's
  new army.
- **Religion and the stranger.** Saying the wrong thing about God, the Prophet or the
  Quran is dangerous in any year, and in 833 it becomes a state matter. Lying about
  the player's own beliefs has its own costs.
- **The caliph's death.** Warn him about the river water and the fresh dates, stay
  silent, or keep away from the frontier. Saving him prolongs the Mihna; his death
  brings the Turkish army and Samarra. The epilogue follows honestly from what the
  player did.

## Medium-difficulty hint examples (incidental, never highlighted)
- A tax clerk tallies a village's land tax on his fingers, then writes the result out
  in words, then checks it on his fingers again.
- A paper seller complains that the good rag paper from Samarkand costs three times
  his own, and his own tears when it is folded.
- A copyist rubs his eyes and says he will be finished with the trade in a year or two:
  the letters swim at close range.
- A physician mentions, in passing, that the patients in the fever ward who were given
  the cheap barley water did as well as those given his expensive syrup.
- A ferryman says the drinking water is sweeter when it has stood a night in the jar.

## People specific to this campaign (fictional; real people are in the era file)
- **Abu Sahl Yazdad**, Persian paper maker in Karkh – a convert, rich from the diwans'
  orders, anxious about quality and about rivals from Samarkand; will pay for any
  improvement to his mill.
- **Maryam**, enslaved copyist in a book dealer's household – Christian, from the
  Byzantine frontier, reads Greek, Syriac and Arabic; quicker than anyone the player
  meets and with fewer choices.
- **Isa ibn Yuhanna**, Nestorian physician's assistant at the bimaristan – trained in
  the Gondeshapur style, loyal to Galen, honest enough to notice when a remedy fails.
- **Rashid al-Turki**, a young Turkish ghulam in al-Mu'tasim's guard – bought in
  Samarkand, ambitious, curious about anything that helps a soldier.
- **Abu Bakr al-Harrani**, a traditionist who copies hadith for a living – pious,
  kind, suspicious of philosophers, and among the first in the city to be questioned
  in 833.

## Evaluation focus
- Digits and al-jabr: did the player get place-value arithmetic from the astronomers'
  tables into the hands of clerks and merchants, and did they add something al-Khwarizmi
  did not have (symbols, negative numbers, decimal fractions)?
- The method: could the player explain a controlled trial or a decisive experiment to
  scholars who revere Galen and Aristotle, and get one done?
- Paper and copying: did they turn paper into cheaper books (block printing, movable
  type), and did they think about who would control it?
- Instruments: lenses, a better clock, a magnetic compass; which did they get built,
  and by whom?
- Health: water, hands, fever wards, the salt in the southern fields.
- The Mihna, slavery and the army: what did their gifts do to the caliph's power, to
  his opponents and to the people who would carry out his orders?
