# Campaign: Rome 46 BC – The Longest Year

- **id:** `rome-46bc`
- **era guide:** `techtree/generated/europe-46bc-rome.md` (compact fallback: `techtree/generated/europe-46bc-rome/briefing.md`; source: `techtree/eras/europe-46bc-rome.yaml`)
- **start date:** early October 46 BC by the old calendar (adjustable)
- **hook:** accident (default) or mission from the future (option below)
- **recommended duration:** one year, or "until the Ides" (to 15 March 44 BC)
- **language:** Latin and Greek; with the language axis off, the player is understood

## Premise
Rome under Caesar, dictator for ten years and just back from Africa. He has held four
triumphs, feasted the city, opened his new forum and is now fixing time itself: this
year is being stretched to 445 days so that the calendar matches the sun again.
Later writers call it the year of confusion. Cleopatra is a guest across the Tiber. The
Republic's offices still exist, and nobody knows what they are worth.

It is the best-engineered city the player could land in before the modern age, with
aqueducts, sewers, concrete and cranes, and one of the worst places to sell a
labour-saving machine: labour is enslaved and cheap. Everything else is missing.
There is no paper, no zero, no stirrup, no compass, no distilled spirit, no idea that
an experiment could overrule a philosopher. Most of the player's easy wins are here,
and most of the game is finding someone with a reason to want them.

## Mission from the future (optional hook)
The message reads: *"He has a year and a half. What he builds in that time lasts two
thousand years. You will be returned in one year."* It does not say whether the
player should save him.

## Opening scene
The player wakes under an arcade at the edge of the Forum. Carpenters are taking down
the stands from the last of the games; a public slave is repainting the calendar
board on a wall, crossing out days and adding a month nobody has heard of. The smell
is of charcoal, frying fish and the drains. A money changer at his table looks at the
stranger's shoes, then at the stranger's hands, deciding whose slave this is.

## Arc (loose, react to the player)
1. **Nobody** – without a patron, a stranger in Rome has no standing and may be taken
   for a runaway slave. First need: a protector (a freedman contractor, a Greek
   physician or teacher, a senator's household) and a claim to be somebody.
2. **A use** – a demonstration that earns a place: reckoning with zero for a
   contractor's accounts, a wheelbarrow or a collar on a building site, a remedy, a
   prediction.
3. **The calendar** – the reform is the open door to the top. The player knows the
   leap-year rule, knows it will be applied wrongly after Caesar's death (every third
   year instead of every fourth), and knows the small error that takes sixteen
   centuries to matter. Whoever says so convincingly is brought to the people around
   the astronomer Sosigenes, and from there to Caesar's staff.
4. **Caesar** – quick, curious, impatient, short of time. He leaves for Spain late in
   the year and returns in autumn 45. He wants things that work: for the army, for
   the grain supply and, from 45 BC, for the Parthian war he begins to plan.
5. **The others** – Cicero, who will listen to any idea and write it down; Varro,
   asked to found the first public library, who has thought about tiny creatures in
   marsh air; the men who will kill Caesar, who are also the ones who talk about law
   and liberty.
6. **The Ides** – if the player stays long enough or the campaign runs to March 44.
   The player knows the day.

## Hard choices
- **The dictator.** Caesar is the fastest way to change anything, and every gift
  strengthens one-man rule. The men who defend the Republic defend an oligarchy of
  slave owners. There is no clean side.
- **The Ides of March.** Warn him, stay silent, or leave. Saving him does not save
  the Republic; his death brings thirteen more years of civil war. The epilogue
  follows honestly from what the player did.
- **Slavery.** It is everywhere, including in the household that shelters the
  player, and the people who could build the player's inventions are often slaves.
  Arguing against it openly is absurd to almost everyone and dangerous. What the
  player does in small things counts (see `modules/content-guidelines.md`).
- **Weapons.** The stirrup, better steel and anything that burns go straight to the
  legions and the Parthian campaign.

## Medium-difficulty hint examples (incidental, never highlighted)
- A contractor complains that his foreman needs half a morning and a board of pebbles
  to check one page of accounts.
- A wine merchant boasts that his best syrup is boiled down in lead, "it gives the
  sweetness copper never does".
- A doctor remarks that the fevers come back every autumn to the low quarters by the
  river and never to the hills.
- A cavalry officer back from Africa rubs his thighs and says a man is only as good
  as his grip on the horse.

## People specific to this campaign (fictional; real people are in the era file)
- **Philargyrus**, Greek freedman and building contractor in the Subura – rich,
  shrewd, despised by his betters; will pay for anything that saves him money.
- **Chrysis**, enslaved secretary in his household – reads Greek and Latin, keeps the
  accounts, learns faster than anyone the player meets; her position is the player's
  first lesson about this world.
- **Marcus Fadius**, centurion on leave, veteran of Gaul – practical, loyal to
  Caesar, sees every device as kit for the legion.
- **Diokles**, physician from Cos with a practice near the cattle market – proud of
  his school, argues well, has never tested a remedy against another.
- **Lucius Aufidius**, junior pontiff's clerk – knows how the calendar was really
  run and who profited from the muddle.

## Evaluation focus
- Zero and place value: could the player teach written arithmetic to people who
  reckon with pebbles, and did they find the buyers (contractors, tax farmers, the
  army's quartermasters)?
- The calendar: did they explain the leap-year rule so that it could not be
  misapplied?
- Which of the cheap mechanical ideas did they get built, and for whom?
- Lead, malaria, water: did they improve health in a city that already has the
  infrastructure but the wrong reasons?
- What did they do with what they knew about 15 March, and what did their gifts do
  to the balance between one man and the Republic?
