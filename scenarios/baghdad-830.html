Baghdad 830 – The House of Wisdom

Abbasid Golden Age · Ahead of Medieval Europe

Play this scenario in ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini (best in voice mode):

“Start a game of blueprintsfromtomorrow.com in Baghdad 830”

Why this era?

Baghdad under al-Ma'mun, the son of Harun al-Rashid, who took the city from his own brother in a siege that burned whole quarters. The caliph collects books, pays translators, sends astronomers up to the roof of the Shammasiyya quarter to check Ptolemy's tables, and argues theology the way other rulers hunt. He has a library, the Khizanat al-Hikma, which later writers will call the House of Wisdom and turn into a university that never existed. The real thing is a palace library, a few librarians, a bench of copyists, and a city full of patrons who pay by the page for Greek turned into Arabic.

This is the only campaign in which the player arrives ahead of Europe and finds the world already reaching for much of what they know. Paper is made here. Indian numerals with a sign for zero have just been written up by al-Khwarizmi, together with a new art he calls al-jabr. There are hospitals, astrolabes, alembics, canals, Euclid in Arabic. What is missing is subtler: lenses, printing, an experiment that may overrule Galen, a clock that runs without water. And time: in three years the caliph will order every judge to swear that the Quran was created, and then die of a fever on the Byzantine frontier.

How people think here

Baghdad, the City of Peace, is not yet seventy years old and is the largest city west of China, with several hundred thousand people (estimates vary widely). It is still repairing the damage of the siege of 812–813, when al-Ma'mun's armies took it from his brother al-Amin. Al-Ma'mun has ruled from here since 819 and spends these years on campaign against Byzantium. God is one and the Quran is His word; whether that word is created or eternal is the question the caliph has made a matter of state. He favours the rational theologians of the Mu'tazila against the traditionists, and in 833 he will make every judge swear to it. Learning is prized, and patrons pay for it. Greek books in philosophy, medicine, mathematics and astronomy are being turned into Arabic, mostly through Syriac and mostly by Christian scholars, beside Persian and Indian works. Aristotle, Galen, Euclid and Ptolemy are authorities to be understood and reconciled, not tested; nobody yet expects an experiment to overrule them. Astrology is a learned profession. Medicine is Galenic and in the hands of Nestorian Christian physicians. Paper is made in the city and is replacing papyrus in the offices, but every book is copied by hand. Indian numerals with a sign for zero are known to a few astronomers and are not yet used by clerks. Christians, Jews and Zoroastrians live under protection and pay the poll tax. Slavery is everywhere, from household servants and singing girls to the Turkish soldiers that the caliph's brother is buying.

Significant events around this time

  • Each summer: the army leaves for the Byzantine frontier; Tigris and Euphrates floods in spring, low water and heat (well over 40 °C) from June to September.
  • 827 (AH 212): al-Ma'mun publicly declares the Quran created and Ali the best of men after the Prophet.
  • c. 827–828: Khalid ibn Abd al-Malik and Ali ibn Isa al-Asturlabi measure a degree of the meridian in the plain of Sinjar, about 56 2/3 Arabic miles (reports give 56 to 57; the date is not fixed).
  • 828 (AH 213): solstice observations at the Shammasiyya quarter of Baghdad under Yahya ibn Abi Mansur; al-Khwarizmi is reported present. Abdallah ibn Tahir becomes governor of Khurasan.
  • 829: Theophilos becomes Byzantine emperor. Observations at Shammasiyya continue into 829–830.
  • 829/831–832 (start disputed): the Bashmurite revolt of Christian Copts in the Nile delta, crushed when the caliph comes to Egypt with al-Afshin.
  • 830 (AH 215) spring–summer: al-Ma'mun leads his first personal campaign into Byzantine territory, the first by a caliph in twenty years, takes Majida by surrender and storms and razes Qurra (July 830); Sundus also falls. Yahya ibn Abi Mansur dies in his train near Aleppo (830, or 832).
  • 831: second campaign; Heraclea Cybistra and Tyana surrender. Observations on Mount Qasiyun at Damascus begin.
  • 831 10 Jul: Zubayda, Harun's widow and al-Amin's mother, dies in Baghdad.
  • 832: the frontier fortress of Loulon falls after a long siege (date disputed); al-Ma'mun in Egypt to end the Bashmurite revolt.
  • 833 spring (AH 218): from Raqqa and the frontier the caliph writes to Ishaq ibn Ibrahim in Baghdad: judges and traditionists must declare the Quran created or lose office. The Mihna begins. Ahmad ibn Hanbal refuses and is sent in chains to the caliph.
  • 833 25 May: al-Abbas ibn al-Ma'mun crosses into Byzantine territory and begins building a fortress at Tyana.
