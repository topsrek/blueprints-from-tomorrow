Why this era?

Rome under Caesar, dictator for ten years and just back from Africa. He has held four triumphs, feasted the city, opened his new forum and is now fixing time itself: this year is being stretched to 445 days so that the calendar matches the sun again. Later writers call it the year of confusion. Cleopatra is a guest across the Tiber. The Republic's offices still exist, and nobody knows what they are worth.

It is the best-engineered city the player could land in before the modern age, with aqueducts, sewers, concrete and cranes, and one of the worst places to sell a labour-saving machine: labour is enslaved and cheap. Everything else is missing. There is no paper, no zero, no stirrup, no compass, no distilled spirit, no idea that an experiment could overrule a philosopher. Most of the player's easy wins are here, and most of the game is finding someone with a reason to want them.

How people think here

Rome is a city of perhaps 700,000 to a million people and the capital of the Mediterranean, ruled in fact by one man: Caesar, dictator for ten years, back from the civil war in Africa. The Republic's forms survive, the Senate, the assemblies, the magistrates, and everyone wonders for how long. The gods are part of the state: augurs and haruspices read birds and entrails before public business, and prodigies are expiated. Educated Romans read Greek and choose among Stoics, Epicureans (who teach atoms and natural causes) and Academic sceptics, without experiment as a method. Medicine is Greek and practised mostly by Greek freedmen and slaves; disease comes from bad air, imbalance or the gods. Engineering is superb and empirical: aqueducts, sewers, roads, concrete, cranes. Labour is cheap because much of it is enslaved, so nobody looks for machines to save it. Numbers are Roman numerals, reckoned on the counting board; books are papyrus rolls copied by hand.

Significant events around this time

  • Every August to October (by the sun, not the drifting calendar): the fever season in Rome and the Campagna; those who can leave for the hills.
  • 46 BC Feb: the regular intercalary month of 23 days is inserted (Caesar as pontifex maximus), bringing the year to 378 days.
  • 46 BC Sep: Caesar's four triumphs (Gaul, Egypt, Pontus, Africa) on four days, with games and public feasts; awnings over the spectators are said to be silk, and Hirrius lends morays for the banquets. On 26 Sep, the last day, he dedicates the temple of Venus Genetrix and the unfinished Forum Iulium.
  • 46 BC autumn: Caesar pardons M. Claudius Marcellus at the Senate's plea; Cicero breaks his silence with a speech of thanks (Pro Marcello).
  • 46 BC: the free-grain list is recounted street by street through the landlords and cut from 320,000 to 150,000; juries limited to senators and equites; a sumptuary law (litters, purple, pearls, delicacies seized in the market); all but the ancient collegia dissolved; citizenship for physicians and teachers of the liberal arts in Rome.
  • 46 BC, between November and December: two extra months totalling 67 days (intercalaris prior and posterior) are inserted; the year has 445 days.
  • 46 BC late: Caesar leaves for Spain. Lepidus, consul and master of horse, runs Rome with six or eight prefects; Balbus and Oppius handle Caesar's business.
  • 46–44 BC: in Syria the Pompeian Q. Caecilius Bassus holds Apamea against Caesar's generals and deals with the Parthians.
  • 45 BC 1 Jan: the Julian calendar begins: 365 days, an extra day in February every fourth year, ten days spread over seven months. Told that Lyra rises tomorrow, Cicero answers: yes, by edict.
  • 45 BC Jan–Feb: Cicero's daughter Tullia gives birth to a son and dies about a month later at Tusculum.
  • 45 BC: Caesar's prefect Gaius Clovius issues coins of brass (orichalcum), a metal new to Roman coinage. Nigidius Figulus dies in exile.
  • 45 BC: Cicero writes the Hortensius, Academica, De finibus and Tusculan Disputations, and begins On the Nature of the Gods, with Archimedes' tomb and the planetary spheres of Archimedes and Posidonius.
